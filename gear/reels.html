Fishing Reels

A reel stores line, helps retrieve it, and uses drag to let a hooked fish pull line under controlled pressure. Choose the reel type first, then size it to your rod, line, lure, and target.

1. Spinning reels: size 1000–6000

How they work

The spool stays still while a rotating bail winds line onto it. They are beginner-friendly and cast light lures well. The size numbers are approximate and vary by manufacturer.

SizeTypical matchExamples
1000–2000Ultralight/light rod and light linePanfish, small trout, small streams and ponds
2500–3000Light/medium all-around freshwater setupTrout, bass, kokanee, and smaller steelhead setups
4000–5000More line capacity and drag for larger fish or currentSteelhead, salmon, larger bass, and river fishing
6000Larger/heavier setup; confirm rod balance and line capacityLarge salmon, strong current, or heavier presentations

A size number is not a universal measurement. Check a reel’s actual line capacity and drag, and balance it with the rod instead of choosing by number alone.

2. Baitcasting reels

Low-profile and round baitcasters

The spool rotates as line leaves it, giving accurate casts and direct control with medium to heavy lures. Magnetic or centrifugal brakes help control spool speed; the thumb controls it during a cast.

Good for: largemouth and smallmouth bass around cover, heavier lures, and some salmon setups. Choose a reel by rod pairing, line capacity, and drag, not spinning-reel size numbers.

3. Fly reels

Match the reel to fly-rod weight

Fly reels hold backing and fly line, retrieve line, and provide drag. Choose a reel labeled for the same line weight as the rod and fly line (for example, a 5-weight reel for a 5-weight outfit). A large arbor retrieves line faster and reduces line memory.

Good for: trout and steelhead when paired with a suitable fly rod, line, and backing. Fly reels are not sized with the 1000–6000 spinning scale.

Reel recommendations are starting points. Check current ODFW regulations and size gear for the water and fish.
